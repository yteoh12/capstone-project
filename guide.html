CP1 roadmap: what your proposal must contain

The proposal is built in chapters. Work on them in this order and share each draft with your supervisor.

Chapter 1 · Introduction

Background, problem statement, objectives, scope and significance. Make sure every objective can be measured and later answered by your methodology.

Chapter 2 · Literature review

Analyse, do not just list, existing work. Compare approaches and end with a clear research gap that justifies your project.

Chapter 3 · Methodology

Research design, tools and technologies, data, and the steps you will follow. Justify each choice and link it to your objectives.

Chapter 4 · Work plan

Gantt chart with tasks, dependencies and milestones, plus the risks and how you will handle them.

Abstract, references, quality

A short abstract of the whole proposal, consistent referencing (training on 23 Oct helps), good structure and proofreading.

Logbook

Weekly entries of meetings, work done, problems and critical reflection, signed by your supervisor. Submitted with the proposal.

Marks follow the official CP1 rubric. Milestones show your progress, but the quality of the work decides the marks.

Develop your Table of Contents

Your table of contents is the outline of your proposal. Agree it with your supervisor first, because it shows whether you have thought through the whole project. Due before 7 Nov 2026.

How to do it

  1. Start from the sample below and keep only the sections that fit your project.
  2. Rename the headings for your own topic (for example, replace [Topic] with your topic).
  3. Add sections you need, such as a use case diagram, a survey, or AI model sections.
  4. Use two or three levels of numbering (Chapter, 1.1, 1.1.1) and keep the numbering consistent.
  5. In Word or Google Docs, use Heading styles so the table of contents and page numbers can be generated automatically later.
  6. Check the flow: problem, then objectives, then literature and gap, then methods, then work plan.
  7. Show it to your supervisor and update it when the structure changes.

Ethics application (required if you collect data from people)


If your project collects data from human subjects (surveys, interviews, focus groups, usability tests, observation, experiments, or any data that identifies people), you must obtain ethics approval before you collect any data. Data collected before approval cannot be used. Use your university’s own ethics forms and procedure; ask your supervisor or coordinator which committee to submit to and the deadlines.


Step 1. Do I need ethics approval?
Tick everything that applies to your project.


Step 2. Ethical principles to build into your design


Voluntary participation
No pressure. People may refuse or withdraw at any time without penalty.


Informed consent
Participants understand the purpose, tasks, time, risks and data use before agreeing.


Minimise harm
Avoid physical, emotional, social or reputational harm. Take extra care with sensitive topics.


Confidentiality and anonymity
Collect only the personal data you need. Remove or protect names and identifiers.


Data protection
Store data securely, limit access, and delete it when no longer needed. Check the personal data protection law that applies to you (in Malaysia, the PDPA 2010).


Honesty and fairness
Do not mislead participants. Be careful with students, minors or other vulnerable groups, and with people who report to you or your supervisor.


Step 3. Prepare your application
  1. Discuss with your supervisor early, ideally while writing Chapter 3.
  2. Fix the exact details: who the participants are, how many, how you recruit them, what they do and for how long.
  3. Write the instruments (survey questions, interview guide, test tasks).
  4. Prepare the Participant Information Sheet and Consent Form (templates below).
  5. Write how you will store, protect and delete the data.
  6. Get your supervisor’s review and signature.
  7. Submit to the ethics committee before the deadline and respond quickly to any comments.
  8. Begin data collection only after you receive written approval, and keep the approval letter and signed forms.


What the application usually asks for
Names and details may differ between universities. Check your own form.


Project summary
Title, researcher, supervisor, aim, and why human participants are needed.
This project aims to [aim]. Data will be collected from [number] [participants] using [method] because [reason it cannot be done without people].


Participants and recruitment
Who is included and excluded, the sample size, how you will invite them, and whether anyone is vulnerable (for example under 18).
Participants are [group] aged [range] who [criteria]. They will be recruited through [channel] and will not be pressured to take part.


Procedure
What participants do, how long it takes, where, and whether you record audio, video or screens.


Risks and benefits
Possible discomfort or harm, how you reduce it, and the benefits to participants or society.
The risk is minimal. Participants may feel [discomfort] when [situation]. This is reduced by [measure], and participants may skip any question or stop at any time.


Consent process
How you explain the study and obtain consent (signed form, or a consent question at the start of an online survey).


Data management
What data you collect, whether it identifies people, where it is stored, who can access it, how long you keep it and how it is destroyed.
Data will be [anonymised] and stored on [secure location] accessible only to the researcher and supervisor. Raw data will be deleted [time] after the project ends.


Attachments
Information sheet, consent form, survey or interview questions, permission letters from organisations, and the supervisor’s endorsement.


Download templates
Plain text, ready to paste into Word or Google Docs. Replace everything in [brackets] and add your university’s logo and committee details.
  


Common mistakes
  • Collecting data before approval, even a “pilot”.
  • Applying too late. Review can take time, so apply early.
  • Consent forms in difficult language, or that hide risks.
  • Collecting names, emails or photos you do not need.
  • Storing data on personal devices or shared drives without protection.
  • Recruiting classmates or your own students under pressure.

How to create your Gantt chart


A Gantt chart shows each task as a bar over time, so you and your supervisor can see what happens when and what depends on what.




List all tasks
Break the project into tasks: literature collection, each chapter, system or experiment design, supervisor reviews, proofreading and submission.


Estimate start and end
Use weeks as your time unit. Be realistic: reviews by your supervisor take time, so leave a buffer before the 8 Jan 2027 deadline.


Show dependencies
Mark which tasks cannot start until another finishes (for example, methodology depends on the literature review and gap).


Add milestones
Mark key points such as SAF upload, chapter drafts, full draft and final upload.


Build it in a tool
Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets (colour cells across week columns), or an online planner. Search “how to make a Gantt chart in Excel” for tutorials.


Update it every week
Bring it to every supervisor meeting and show what is done, late or changed.

Collect relevant literature


Gather scholarly articles, books and reports that relate to your project. Start with these search tools, then use the academic databases below for peer-reviewed sources:



Google ScholarBroad search across papers, theses and citations.
Semantic ScholarAI-assisted search with paper summaries.
LitmapsVisual maps of how papers cite each other.
SciSpaceSearch papers and get help understanding them.
ResearchRabbitDiscover related papers and authors.


Academic databases
Search these for peer-reviewed journal and conference papers. Many need your university login, so access them through the Library website when you are off campus.



ScopusLarge database of journals and conferences, with citation counts.
IEEE XploreEngineering, computing and electronics papers and standards.
ScienceDirectElsevier journals and books across science and technology.


Tips for good sources
Prefer recent (about the last 5 years) peer-reviewed papers, plus a few classic foundational ones. Combine search tools (to discover papers quickly) with Scopus, IEEE Xplore and ScienceDirect (to find and download peer-reviewed full texts). If you cannot open a paper, log in through the Library or ask a librarian. Check the Library’s Information Skills and Referencing training notes. Save every source with its full citation as you go, so referencing is easy later. Always check the AI summaries against the original paper.



Literature review and literature synthesis


Organise and analyse your sources in a table (Excel or Google Sheets) to compare them and find themes, methods and gaps.


Learn how
Watch “How to Write a Literature Review: 3 Minute Step-by-step Guide” from Scribbr, and the video on research and synthesis tables recommended by your supervisor.


Search the Scribbr video Search synthesis table videos


From table to writing
  1. Read each paper and fill one row of the synthesis table.
  2. Look down the columns to group papers into themes.
  3. Compare methods, results and limitations across papers.
  4. Write each theme as a paragraph that compares sources, not one paragraph per paper.
  5. End with the gap: what is missing, and how your project addresses it.

FAQ


How often should I meet my supervisor?
Regularly through Weeks 3 to 14. Bring your Gantt chart, new drafts and logbook each time.


What if I cannot find my supervisor’s name?
Check the supervisor-student list in eLearn (posted at the end of Week 3), then contact your coordinator.


Can I submit late?
No. Without submission or late submission, students are marked as ZERO. Upload early and check that the files open.


Do tools like AI summarisers count as sources?
No. Use them to find and understand papers, then cite the original papers, not the tool. Always verify what they tell you.
